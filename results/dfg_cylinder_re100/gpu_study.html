Summary ⚠ Usable, with caveats

Question. On a cylinder that sheds vortices, does the project's GPU program gpucfd reach the accuracy of OpenFOAM's grid study in less time, what does it cost, and what holds it back?

gpucfd met the target we set before the runs. Its drag was \(0.09\,\%\) and \(0.16\,\%\) below the published reference (two positions of the cylinder), and its three-grid study took about 1,005 s (with the implicit time scheme on the finest grid; 1,421 s without it), against about 1,743 s for OpenFOAM's four-grid study. But the target is easier than it looks. The published reference is itself about \(0.2\,\%\) too low. Against the value it is heading for, gpucfd's drag is \(0.31\,\%\) to \(0.38\,\%\) low, and OpenFOAM's is within \(0.04\,\%\). And OpenFOAM is still cheaper for this accuracy: it reaches the same drag accuracy in 153 s.

Key findings

  • Did gpucfd meet the target? Yes, as it was defined: drag within \(0.25\,\%\) of the reference, in less time than OpenFOAM's whole study. It got \(0.09\,\%\) and \(0.16\,\%\), in 1,421 s and 1,375 s (explicit scheme; 1,005 s with the implicit scheme on the finest grid), against about 1,743 s.
  • Is that a fair test of accuracy? Not quite. The reference was itself computed on finer and finer grids, and its drag was still rising. Its three finest values point to \(3.1952\), which is \(0.21\,\%\) above the number we compare with (\(3.1884\)). Against that, gpucfd is \(0.31\,\%\) to \(0.38\,\%\) low and OpenFOAM is within \(0.04\,\%\). So this result does not show that gpucfd is as accurate as OpenFOAM.
  • Was the good drag luck? Partly. gpucfd cuts the cylinder out of a grid of rows and columns, and where the cuts fall changes the answer a little. With the cylinder moved half a cell the drag went from \(0.09\,\%\) to \(0.16\,\%\) low. So what can be quoted is a range. Two positions are not a proper spread.
  • gpucfd costs far less per cell and step. Why is it not much quicker? Per cell and step it is \(25\times\) to \(45\times\) cheaper than OpenFOAM. But its finest run has \(2.1\times\) the cells and \(6.5\times\) the steps, so it is only \(1.8\times\) quicker (1,180 s against 2,110 s). And OpenFOAM's second grid, with 13,432 cells, already has the drag.
  • Does an implicit time scheme make gpucfd faster? Yes, but not more accurate. It gives the same answer (drag and frequency within \(0.00047\,\%\), lift within \(0.014\,\%\), of the explicit runs). The finest run takes 764 s instead of 1,180 s, and the study 1,005 s instead of 1,421 s. We had expected 550 to 800 s: that was missed. The drag is as far from the reference as before (\(-0.09\,\%\)).
  • What holds gpucfd back? Three things (section 5). Its explicit time steps are very small: on the finest grid \(3.3\times\) smaller than the flow alone needs. The implicit scheme takes \(3.3\times\) fewer steps, but each costs \(2.1\times\) more, so the run gains \(1.55\times\), not \(3.3\times\). A finer grid needs the pressure worked out more exactly, which makes each explicit step \(2.1\times\) dearer; the implicit scheme did not relax that setting on the finest grid. And small cells cannot go in one place only: just \(21\,\%\) of the clustered cells are in the band around the cylinder. The finest grid still takes \(76\,\%\) of the study's time.
  • Why were lift and frequency worse on the clustered grid at first? The small cells stopped too soon behind the cylinder, where the vortices form. With the small cells reaching \(4\) diameters behind it instead of \(1.5\), the lift maximum went from \(-5.15\,\%\) to \(-3.68\,\%\) and the frequency from \(+0.54\,\%\) to \(+0.25\,\%\), as good as equal cells. But then the clustered study took 308 s against 328 s: fewer cells bought almost no time.
  • Why the verdict is 'usable, with caveats': measured against the value the reference is heading for, gpucfd's drag is outside the target's tolerance; the finest clustered grid and OpenFOAM disagree by more than their error estimates allow in: lift minimum; the finest equal-cell grid and OpenFOAM disagree by more than their error estimates allow in: lift maximum; by the case's own rules the equal-cell study is rated WARN; the finest clustered grid is outside its own error band in: lift minimum.
LimitationOnly the drag is close. The lift maximum is \(0.85\,\%\) to \(1.15\,\%\) low and the frequency \(0.10\,\%\) to \(0.13\,\%\) high. OpenFOAM: \(+0.41\,\%\) and \(-0.12\,\%\).
Limitationgpucfd cannot prove its own accuracy yet. Its error estimate for the drag is \(0.62\,\%\) to \(0.75\,\%\), which is \(2.5\) to \(3.0\) times the target. The last step also fell faster than the one before it (it would be \(-0.31\,\%\) on the earlier trend).
LimitationThe comparison is not like for like. Different kinds of grid, four grids against three, a processor against a graphics card, OpenFOAM's implicit time scheme against gpucfd's explicit (or, on the finest grid, partly implicit) one, one timed run each on one PC (section 1).
LimitationThe implicit-scheme runs are one cylinder position, one timed run each. The study time 1,005 s has no spread, and the longer-band run (\(-0.69\,\%\) lift maximum) is a hint, not a result.
LimitationOpenFOAM's study time is partly an estimate. Three of its four runs were really made with the stop rule; the finest, which is \(81\,\%\) of the time, was not.
LimitationTuning time is not counted on either side. For gpucfd that is 436 s of pressure checks and a first run of the finest grid that never settled (2,680 s).
LimitationOne flow only: two dimensions, Reynolds number 100, one cylinder, one PC.
RecommendationStill open. (1) The longer band of small cells was run once on the finest grid (implicit scheme, one cylinder position, \(25\,\%\) more cells): the lift maximum went from \(-0.85\,\%\) to \(-0.69\,\%\). That is less than moving the cylinder changes it (\(0.3\,\%\)), so it is a hint. (2) The next gain in speed: one pressure solve per time step in place of three (gpucfd/DESIGN.md, 'IMEX with immersed bodies, stage 3'). (3) More positions of the cylinder, to turn the range into a spread. For choosing a tool today: for a round body like this, OpenFOAM with a grid that follows the body is still the cheaper way to a given accuracy.

1 What is compared

The problem: a fluid flows along a channel past a round cylinder (case 4 of this project). Vortices leave the cylinder alternately from its two sides, so the forces on it swing up and down. We solve it with two programs: OpenFOAM, the established program, on the processor, and gpucfd, the project's own program, on the graphics card. Each answer is checked against numbers published by another research group. The list below explains the words used; Figure 1 shows gpucfd's two kinds of grid.

Words used on this page

  • Reference. Numbers for this exact problem published by another research group (FeatFlow, TU Dortmund), computed on very fine grids. We use them as the answer to compare with. They are not exact either.
  • Drag, lift. The force on the cylinder along the flow (drag) and across it (lift). Lift swings up and down as vortices leave the cylinder; we compare its largest value.
  • Shedding frequency. How often vortices leave the cylinder. Written as a pure number, the Strouhal number.
  • Cell, grid. The flow is calculated on a grid of small boxes called cells. More and smaller cells give a more exact answer but cost more time.
  • Cells across the diameter. How many cells fit across the cylinder's width at the cylinder. It is the fairest way to compare grids of different shapes.
  • OpenFOAM grid. A grid that bends around the cylinder (grids M1 to M4, each twice as fine as the one before).
  • Equal cells. gpucfd's first kind of grid: all cells the same size, over the whole channel (G1 to G3).
  • Clustered cells. gpucfd's second kind: small cells in a band around the cylinder, larger ones far away (C1 to C3).
  • Cut out of the grid. gpucfd's grids cannot bend, so the round cylinder is cut out of the rows and columns (an 'immersed body'). Where the cuts fall changes the answer a little.
  • Check runs. Extra runs to test our own result: C1s to C3s (cylinder moved half a cell), C2w (a longer band of small cells behind the cylinder), and the equal-cell study repeated with a stricter pressure setting.
  • Time step. The calculation moves forward in small jumps of time. Smaller jumps mean more steps and more work.
  • Explicit and implicit. An explicit time scheme makes each jump simple and quick, but it is only stable if the jump is very small. An implicit one costs more per jump and allows larger jumps. OpenFOAM's is implicit. gpucfd's first scheme is explicit; it now also has one that treats friction implicitly (IMEX), which works with a body in the flow (section 5).
  • Pressure setting. At every step the pressure is worked out approximately. The setting says how closely. Too loose gives noisy forces; too strict wastes time.
  • Error estimate. What a study says about its own error without looking at the reference. Made from three grids of growing fineness (the grid convergence index).
  • Order. How fast the error shrinks when the cells are halved. Order 2 means four times smaller; order 1.5 about 2.8 times.
  • Study. A grid study: the problem solved on several grids, finest last. Its time is the time of all its runs together.
  • Periodic. The flow has settled into a steady repeating pattern; only then are the forces measured.
  • Wall time. Elapsed clock time, as a person would measure it with a stopwatch.

Rules for a fair comparison

  • Same problem, same measurements. Both programs solve the same channel and cylinder. Forces and frequency are taken by the same code from the last 4 cycles and compared with the same reference.
  • Each tool with its own best settings. OpenFOAM: a grid that follows the cylinder, with the time scheme, pressure solver and number of threads chosen by tests in the main report. gpucfd: its two kinds of grid, with the pressure setting chosen for each grid by one rule.
  • Same procedure. The coarsest grid starts from rest and runs \(15\) s of flow. Each finer grid starts from the coarser grid's flow. A run ends when the flow repeats from cycle to cycle.
  • Rules written before the runs: how accuracy is judged, what counts as the same answer, the target, and how each check run would be read (numerics.yaml, gpucfd/DESIGN.md, ROADMAP.md).
  • Time is elapsed time of every run in a study, including sampling the forces. Tuning runs are not counted.
  • Not like for like: grids. The two kinds of program need different grids, so cell counts cannot be compared directly, and OpenFOAM's study has four grids where gpucfd's have three.
  • Not like for like: hardware. gpucfd uses the graphics card and one processor thread. OpenFOAM uses 1, 1, 4, 16 processor threads on its four grids.
  • Not like for like: OpenFOAM's study time. OpenFOAM's runs were recorded with fixed lengths (2,548 s). gpucfd's stop when the flow repeats. To compare the same thing, OpenFOAM's time with that stop was worked out from its recorded forces: 1,743 s. Three of its four runs were then really made with the stop (M1 30 s (estimate 25 s), M2 90 s (estimate 87 s), M3 211 s (estimate 223 s)); the finest was not.
  • Not like for like: time-step check. OpenFOAM's time-step error was measured on its second grid. gpucfd's was measured once, on its middle equal-cell grid (halving the step changed the drag by \(3 \times 10^{-5}\)); none was made on the clustered grids.

2 Method

What was decided, why, and what was changed after the first results.

M1
Compare whole studies, not single runs
Why this choice
Someone without a reference needs several grids to know how large the error is. So the cost that counts is the cost of the whole study.
Alternatives
The cheapest single run that reaches an accuracy. Reported too (OpenFOAM's second grid).
M2
The target, fixed in advance: mean and maximum drag within \(0.25\,\%\) of the reference, in less time than OpenFOAM's whole study (about 1,740 s)
Why this choice
OpenFOAM's finest grid is about \(0.25\,\%\) from the reference, so this means 'as accurate as OpenFOAM's study'. Its study time is what gpucfd has to beat to be worth using.
Alternatives
A target on lift or frequency too (not set; both are reported).
M3
gpucfd's grids: equal cells with \(17.6\), \(35.1\), \(70.2\) cells across the diameter, then clustered cells with \(35\), \(70\), \(140\)
Why this choice
With equal cells the whole channel pays for the cell size the cylinder needs. Clustering puts the small cells near the cylinder only: the finest clustered grid has twice the resolution at the cylinder for about the same number of cells (458,752 against 442,368).
Alternatives
Equal cells at \(140\) across: 1,767,920 cells, not run. A grid that bends around the cylinder: gpucfd does not have one.
M4
A run ends when the flow repeats itself, checked while it runs
Why this choice
The first gpucfd runs used OpenFOAM's fixed \(3\) s per grid and were still drifting at the end, because gpucfd's coarsest grid is far from the answer. Changed after those first runs.
Alternatives
Fixed run lengths (OpenFOAM's recorded runs).
M5
One rule for the pressure setting on every grid: the loosest setting whose drag and lift stay within \(10^{-4}\) of the strictest run
Why this choice
At first the setting was tuned on the coarsest grid only, and the finest clustered grid then never settled. Changed after that run: checked on each grid, and runs that had used a setting the rule rejects were repeated.
Alternatives
One strict setting everywhere (\(2.1\times\) the cost per step on the finest grid).
M6
Accuracy and 'same answer' are judged by the case's own rules
Why this choice
A result agrees with the reference if it is closer than the two uncertainties added: the study's own error estimate plus the reference's. Two programs agree if they differ by less than their two error estimates added. Both rules were written before the equal-cell runs; applying the second to the clustered grids is done here.
Alternatives
Agreement to a fixed per cent (ignores how well each side knows its own answer).
M7
Three check runs, each with its question and its reading written before it ran
Why this choice
The first clustered result was better than the trend of the coarser grids. Before quoting it: is it luck (move the cylinder), is the time comparison fair (the pressure rule on the equal-cell grids), and why are lift and frequency worse (a longer band).
Alternatives
More positions of the cylinder: each costs another whole study, about 1,375 s (not run).
M8
Try the implicit time scheme, with its pass rule fixed beforehand: the same answer as the explicit run on the same grid to within \(0.05\,\%\) (drag, frequency) and \(0.3\,\%\) (lift)
Why this choice
On the finest clustered grid the explicit steps were \(3.3\times\) smaller than the flow needs and that grid took \(83\,\%\) of the study's time. Each grid may use whichever scheme is quicker on it, because both solve the same equations.
Alternatives
Halving the step on a grid that misses the rule, to tell a time error from a different answer (not needed: no grid missed).

An implicit treatment of friction removes the limit, and gpucfd now has one that works with a body in the flow: it takes 11,817 steps on the finest grid, but each costs more (last two tables). OpenFOAM, which is implicit, takes 6,000 steps on its finest grid where explicit gpucfd takes 39,232.
