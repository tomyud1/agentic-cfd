Key findings

  • Verdict by the rules written before the runs: FAIL. 16 of 18 rules hold. Broken, FAIL: mean pressure error on the finest grid. Broken, WARN: extra up-and-down of density and pressure (wiggles), every grid of the family.
  • The simulation converges to the exact solution. From 100 to 12800 cells the mean error falls by \(54\times\) (\(\rho\)), \(70\times\) (\(u\)), \(50\times\) (\(p\)). On the finest grid the uniform states are within \(0.0022\,\%\), the shock within \(0.06\) cells and the border of the two gases within \(0.35\) cells of their exact places.
  • It converges more slowly than the pressure limit assumed. The limit was sized for an order of \(0.85\); the measured order over the eight grids is \(0.78\). The pressure error on the finest grid is \(0.0068\,\%\) of \(p_L\), the limit \(0.0055\,\%\). The error sits in the expansion fan: \(81\,\%\) of the pressure error and \(51\,\%\) of the density error on the finest grid, falling with order \(0.78\) there. A smaller time step does not reduce it (see the time-step study).
  • Mass, momentum and energy are kept to round-off on every step of every run (largest relative change \(5.3 \times 10^{-14}\)). This is why the shock sits in the right place on every grid.
  • Sharpness. The shock is \(1.8\) to \(2.2\) cells wide on every grid, so its width in metres halves with the cell size. The border of the two gases is wider, \(4.4\) to \(6.3\) cells: nothing in the flow pushes it back together once it is smeared.
  • The time step matters more than the tutorial suggests. At the tutorial's limit (twice the step used here) pressure and velocity between the fan and the shock ripple by \(3.1\,\%\) on 400 cells; at the step used here by \(0.6\,\%\). The ripple does not go away with a finer grid: it is \(0.6\,\%\) on 12800 cells.
  • Choice of limiter (400 cells): the tutorial's van Leer limiter has the lowest density error of the six combinations tried. The first-order scheme smears the border of the two gases over \(21\) cells; SuperBee keeps it to \(2.4\) cells but doubles the error in the fan.
LimitationOne of the limits set before the runs is not met (pressure on the finest grid), so the verdict is FAIL. The limit rested on my expectation that pressure would converge with an order of at least \(0.85\). Whether the measured \(0.78\) is a property of this solver's scheme or of any method of this kind was not settled: only one solver was run. A second, independent solver on the same grids would tell.
LimitationThe time step is not converged. On 400 cells the density profile changes by \(0.061\,\%\) of \(\rho_L\) when the step is cut to a quarter, against a total error of \(0.13\,\%\). The error against the exact solution is lowest at the step used here because the time error and the space error partly cancel, not because the time error is small.
LimitationThe solver's only time scheme (explicit Euler) leaves a ripple of about \(0.6\,\%\) behind the shock at this time step, and the extra up-and-down of the profiles grows slowly with the number of cells (\(2.8\,\%\) to \(5.5\,\%\)).
LimitationThe grid study was made at one Courant number. The order of convergence at a smaller time step was not measured.
LimitationThe problem is one-dimensional, without viscosity, and ends before any wave reaches a wall. Reflections, boundary layers and the bursting of a real diaphragm are outside it.

1 Problem

A closed tube of air is divided by a thin wall. The air on the left is at ten times the pressure and eight times the density of the air on the right, and all of it is at rest. At time zero the wall is removed. Find how the air moves in the first half millisecond, before any wave reaches an end of the tube, and compare the simulation with the exact solution of this problem.

What we want to find

  1. aDensity, velocity and pressure along the tube at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\), compared with the exact solution
  2. bThe uniform states between the waves at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\): the pressure and the velocity there, and the density on each side of the border between the gas from the left and the gas from the right
  3. cWhere each wave is at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\), and how fast it moves
  4. dTotal mass and total energy of the air in the tube: do they stay constant up to \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\)?
  5. eHow many cells wide each computed jump is, and how the error of the profiles falls as the grid is refined

Setup

  • A straight tube of length \(L\) with closed ends, filled with air at rest. Coordinate \(x\) along the tube, from \(0\) to \(L\). The flow depends only on \(x\) and on time \(t\).
  • A thin wall at \(x = L/2\) separates two uniform states. Left of it: pressure \(p_L\), density \(\rho_L\). Right of it: pressure \(p_R\), density \(\rho_R\).
  • At \(t = 0\) the wall is removed instantly.
  • The air is an ideal gas, \(p = \rho R T\), with a constant ratio of specific heats \(\gamma\).
  • No viscosity and no heat conduction (the Euler equations). The tube wall and the closed ends are frictionless and let no heat through.
  • This is the test problem of Sod (1978) in SI units: in units of \(\rho_L\), \(p_L\) and \(L\) the two states are \((\rho, u, p) = (1, 0, 1)\) and \((0.125, 0, 0.1)\).
  • The problem has an exact solution (the Riemann problem of gas dynamics). It holds until the first wave reaches an end of the tube.

3 Theory

This problem has an exact solution, so the simulation can be checked against it everywhere, not only at a few numbers. The solution is put together from three pieces, each proven by SymPy below, and one equation for the pressure between the waves, solved numerically in two independent ways. The assembled solution is checked once more as a whole: its mass, momentum and energy at the time of interest equal what the tube started with. One thing theory also says about the simulation: a method that captures jumps over a few cells cannot reach the accuracy it has in smooth flow, so the error should fall roughly in proportion to the cell size, not to its square (see Comparison).

Governing equations: mass, momentum and energy of a gas without friction or heat conduction, in one dimension
\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\ \rho u\\ E\end{pmatrix} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\begin{pmatrix}\rho u\\ \rho u^2 + p\\ u(E+p)\end{pmatrix} = 0,\qquad E = \frac{p}{\gamma-1} + \frac12\rho u^2 \]
These are the Euler equations. \(E\) is the energy per unit volume. Written like this, each line says: what a slice of the tube holds changes only by what flows in and out through its two faces. That stays true across a jump, where derivatives do not exist.
No length or time scale: the solution depends on \((x - x_0)/t\) only
\[ \rho,\ u,\ p = \text{functions of }\ \xi = \frac{x - x_0}{t},\qquad x_0 = \frac{L}{2} \]
Two uniform states meeting at a point have no length scale, so the picture at a later time is the earlier one stretched. Every wave leaves the wall position at a constant speed. Three waves form: an expansion fan to the left, a shock to the right and, between them, the border of the two gases.
Left: the expansion fan (a smooth drop in pressure and density)
\[ s = \frac{2}{\gamma+1} - \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}\,\frac{\xi}{c_L},\qquad \rho = \rho_L\,s^{\frac{2}{\gamma-1}},\quad u = \frac{2}{\gamma+1}\,(c_L + \xi),\quad p = p_L\,s^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\qquad (-c_L \le \xi \le u^* - c^*_L) \]
\(s\) is the local speed of sound divided by \(c_L\). The velocity rises linearly across the fan. SymPy checks below that these three functions satisfy the three equations above.
Right: the shock (a jump), from conservation across it
\[ \frac{\rho^*_R}{\rho_R} = \frac{(\gamma+1)\,p^*/p_R + (\gamma-1)}{(\gamma-1)\,p^*/p_R + (\gamma+1)},\qquad S = c_R\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma+1)\,p^*/p_R + (\gamma-1)}{2\gamma}},\qquad u^* = S\left(1 - \frac{\rho_R}{\rho^*_R}\right) \]
These are the Rankine-Hugoniot relations for a shock moving into gas at rest. SymPy checks below that mass, momentum and energy flowing into the jump equal what flows out.
Between them: one pressure and one speed; the density jumps at the border of the two gases
\[ p = p^*,\quad u = u^*\quad (x_{\mathrm{tail}} < x < x_{\mathrm{shock}}),\qquad \rho = \rho^*_L\ \ (x < x_{\mathrm{contact}}),\quad \rho = \rho^*_R\ \ (x > x_{\mathrm{contact}}) \]
\(p^*\) follows from requiring that the fan and the shock give the same \(u^*\) (first card below).
  • fan: mass equation \(\partial_t \rho + \partial_x(\rho u) = 0\)
  • fan: momentum equation \(\partial_t(\rho u) + \partial_x(\rho u^2 + p) = 0\)
  • fan: energy equation \(\partial_t E + \partial_x(u(E + p)) = 0\)
  • fan: each ray \(x - x_0 = \xi t\) moves at the speed of a left-running sound wave, \(\xi = u - c\)
  • fan: no entropy is made, \(p/\rho^\gamma = p_L/\rho_L^\gamma\)
  • fan: it starts at the undisturbed gas, \(\rho = \rho_L,\ u = 0\) at \(\xi = -c_L\) (\(s = 1\))
  • fan: written with \(s = c/c_L\), the velocity is \(u = 2c_L(1 - s)/(\gamma - 1)\)
  • shock: momentum is conserved across the jump, \(p_1 + \rho_1 S^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 (S - u_2)^2\)
  • shock: energy is conserved across the jump, \(h_1 + \tfrac12 S^2 = h_2 + \tfrac12 (S - u_2)^2\) with \(h = \gamma p/((\gamma-1)\rho)\)
  • shock: the gas speed behind it is \(u_2 = (p_2 - p_1)\sqrt{A/(p_2 + B)}\), \(A = 2/((\gamma+1)\rho_1)\), \(B = (\gamma-1)p_1/(\gamma+1)\)
  • pressure between the waves: SymPy (30 digits) and SciPy (bracketing) agree to \(10^{-12}\) of \(p_L\)
  • wave pattern: \(p_R < p^* < p_L\), so the left wave is an expansion fan and the right wave a shock
  • the gas speed behind the shock equals the gas speed behind the fan (one \(u^*\)), to \(10^{-12}\)
  • the fan ends at the left middle state: at its last ray \(\rho = \rho^*_L\), \(u = u^*\), \(p = p^*\)
  • the shock compresses and heats: entropy rises, \(p^*/\rho_R^{*\gamma} > p_R/\rho_R^\gamma\)
  • whole solution at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\): total mass equals the starting mass, to \(10^{-12}\)
  • whole solution at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\): total energy equals the starting energy, to \(10^{-12}\)
  • whole solution at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\): total momentum equals the push of the end walls, \((p_L - p_R)\,t_{\mathrm{end}}\), to \(10^{-12}\)
  • no wave has reached an end of the tube at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\): fan head at \(x > 0\), shock at \(x < L\)

Physical model
D1
The Euler equations in one dimension: an ideal gas with a constant ratio of specific heats, no viscosity, no heat conduction, no turbulence model
Physics
Why this choice
This is the problem as stated, and the exact solution is a solution of these equations. The viscosity is set to zero (mu 0); the solver then skips its viscous and heat-conduction steps. The specific heat is \(c_p = \gamma R/(\gamma - 1)\), so that the gas has exactly the given \(\gamma\). Tref 0 makes the internal energy \(c_v T\) with no offset, so the solver's total energy is the physical one, \(p/(\gamma-1) + \tfrac12\rho u^2\) per unit volume.
Effect on the result
Defines the equations solved. Real air has a small viscosity, which would give the shock a thickness of well under a micrometre here: far below any cell, so it could not be seen.
Alternatives
A viscous gas (the Navier-Stokes equations): not what the exact solution describes.
OpenFOAM settingsconstant/thermophysicalProperties/thermoType/*constant/thermophysicalProperties/mixture/thermodynamics/*constant/thermophysicalProperties/mixture/transport/*constant/turbulenceProperties/simulationType
D2
The molar mass is set so that OpenFOAM's gas constant is exactly the given \(R\); OpenFOAM's universal gas constant is measured by a one-step run, not typed in
Physics
Why this choice
OpenFOAM takes the molar mass and divides its own universal gas constant by it. That constant is built into the program from two rounded numbers, so its digits are not the textbook ones. run.py advances a 10-cell case by one step and reads the constant back from an undisturbed cell (\(p W/(\rho T)\)). The pre-flight then checks that the solver's start densities are the given ones to 12 digits.
Effect on the result
The start densities and \(\gamma\). With the tutorial's rounded molar mass (28.96) the densities would be off by about 0.03 % and every comparison with the exact solution would carry that offset.
Alternatives
The tutorial's rounded value; a textbook value of the universal gas constant.

Solver
D3
rhoCentralFoam, starting from OpenFOAM's own tutorial of this problem (compressible/rhoCentralFoam/shockTube)
Solver
Why this choice
A flow with shocks needs a method that keeps mass, momentum and energy exactly (so that a captured shock moves at the right speed) and that does not ring at a jump. rhoCentralFoam is OpenFOAM's solver built for that: it advances density, momentum and energy directly, with fluxes that need no knowledge of the wave pattern. gpucfd does not apply: it solves flows of constant density.
Effect on the result
Which equations are advanced and how; the exact solution it converges to is the same for any conservative method.
Alternatives
rhoPimpleFoam or sonicFoam (pressure-based, implicit: built for lower speeds, and they smear shocks more); a solver that splits each face into its waves (a Riemann solver, not part of standard OpenFOAM).
OpenFOAM settingssystem/controlDict/application

D4
The whole tube, \(0 \le x \le L\), one cell across with empty sides (a one-dimensional model); closed ends: no flow through them (slip), zero gradient for pressure and temperature
Physics
Why this choice
The flow depends on \(x\) and \(t\) only, and the tube wall is frictionless, so one cell across loses nothing. A frictionless end lets no gas through and no heat: velocity zero normal to it, no gradient of temperature. The cross-section is \(1\,\mathrm{m} \times 1\,\mathrm{m}\), so that a volume integral is the value per unit of cross-section.
Effect on the result
Sets the problem. No wave reaches an end before the time of interest (Theory), so the end conditions are only there to close the tube; the conservation check confirms that nothing leaks through them.
Alternatives
Open ends as in the tutorial (zero gradient for everything): the same answer until a wave arrives, but gas could then leave, and mass would no longer be a check.

The tutorial's choice. Each cell value is extended to its faces with a slope (second order where the flow is smooth); the limiter flattens the slope next to a jump so that no new peak appears. Time stepping
D8
Explicit Euler steps of fixed size: the time of interest divided by 1.8 steps per cell (180 steps on 100 cells, 23040 on 12800), a Courant number of 0.19 on every grid, half the tutorial's step
Accuracy
Why this choice
The solver is explicit: a cell is updated from its neighbours' old values, which is only stable if no signal crosses a cell in one step. The fastest signal of the exact solution is \(u^* + c^*_R = 693\,\mathrm{m/s}\) (Theory); with 1.8 steps per cell it crosses 0.19 of a cell per step. This is the usual Courant number, \((|u| + c)\,\Delta t/\Delta x\). The solver prints half of it (0.097), because it counts differently, so the tutorial's limit of 0.2 is a usual Courant number of about 0.4. The time-step study of the pre-flight tested that step and three smaller ones on 400 cells: at the tutorial's step the pressure behind the shock rings by 2 % and its error doubles; at the step chosen here the ringing is 0.3 %. A fixed step (not the tutorial's adjustable one) makes every run end exactly at the time of interest, writes the snapshots at exact times, and keeps the Courant number the same on every grid, so that the grid study changes one thing only.
Effect on the result
The time error, the ringing behind the shock and a part of the smearing. Euler steps are first order in time, the only choice this solver has. The time error is not negligible: see the time-step study in Comparison.
Alternatives
The tutorial's adjustable step with its limit of 0.2 (rings, and the output times are then not exact). A step two or four times smaller (less ringing, 2 to 4 times the cost, no gain in the error against the exact solution; both tested).

Why this choice
A finite-volume solver holds the average of density, momentum and energy over each cell, not the value at its centre. In a cell that a jump passes through, the two differ by a large part of the jump. So the exact solution is integrated over each cell (in closed form, Theory), and velocity and pressure are formed from those averages as the solver forms them. The reported errors. With point values instead of cell averages every jump would add an error of up to half a cell that the solver did not make.

Point values of the exact solution at the cell centres (simpler, biased at jumps).

7 Comparison

Verification: every run is compared with the exact solution of the same equations, averaged over the run's own cells.

How the differences are measured

Mean error (the L1 norm)
\[ e_q = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|q_i - \bar q_i^{\,\mathrm{exact}}|}{q_{\mathrm{ref}}},\qquad q_{\mathrm{ref}} = \rho_L,\ u^*,\ p_L \]
The mean absolute difference over the cells between the simulation and the exact solution averaged over the same cell. This is the measure to use with jumps: the largest difference never falls, because a cell inside a smeared jump is always wrong by a part of the jump.
Observed order
\[ p = \frac{\ln(e_{\mathrm{coarse}}/e_{\mathrm{fine}})}{\ln 2} \]
\(p = 1\): halving the cells halves the error. \(p = 2\) would cut it by four.
Width of a jump
\[ \frac{|x_{90\%} - x_{10\%}|}{\Delta x} \]
Distance between the points where the profile has made 10 % and 90 % of the jump, in cells. The same rule applied to the exact cell averages gives \(0.9\) to \(1.6\) cells: that is the rule's own floor.
Wiggles
\[ \frac{\sum_i |q_{i+1} - q_i|}{q_L - q_R} - 1,\qquad q = \rho,\ p \]
Zero for a profile that only falls from left to right, as the exact density and pressure do.
Ripple between the fan and the shock
\[ \frac{\max q - \min q}{\mathrm{mean}\ q},\qquad q = p,\ u \]
Over the middle half of the stretch between the back of the fan and the shock, where the exact pressure and velocity are constant.

Order of convergence. Halving the cells cuts the mean error by about \(1.76\times\), not by 2 or 4: orders \(0.81\) (density), \(0.85\) (velocity), \(0.78\) (pressure) over the eight grids (Figure 6). Second order is out of reach with jumps in the solution: a jump spread over a fixed number of cells leaves an error in proportion to the cell size. The surprise is that the order stays below 1.
Where the error is. Not at the shock. On the finest grid the expansion fan holds \(81\,\%\) of the pressure error, \(56\,\%\) of the velocity error and \(51\,\%\) of the density error, and its part falls with order \(0.78\) (pressure), steadily over all grids. The fan is smooth inside, but its two ends are corners, and the scheme rounds them. The limit for the pressure was sized on the assumption that the shock sets the pressure error (order 1 expected, 0.85 allowed); the fan sets it. The shock's own part does fall with order \(0.94\). The slowest part is the stretch around the border of the two gases, where the ripple is: order \(0.54\) for the pressure. It is \(10\,\%\) of the pressure error on the finest grid and would take over on still finer ones.
The time step is not the cause. In the time-step study the fan's part of the pressure error is \(0.12\,\%\), \(0.068\,\%\), \(0.072\,\%\), \(0.08\,\%\) at Courant numbers 0.39, 0.19, 0.10, 0.05: below the step of the grid study it does not fall, it rises a little. I first suspected the ripple behind the shock; the split by wave shows that the ripple is a small part of the error.
The limiter is the cause, in part. With the same grid and time step the density error in the fan is \(0.058\,\%\) (vanLeer), \(0.082\,\%\) (vanLeer, Tadmor flux), \(0.059\,\%\) (vanAlbada), \(0.072\,\%\) (Minmod), \(0.11\,\%\) (SuperBee), \(0.41\,\%\) (upwind). No combination that OpenFOAM offers here does clearly better than the tutorial's.
Time-step error. The error against the exact solution is lowest at the step used for the grid study and rises for smaller steps (Figure 7). So the step is not converged: at a quarter of it the density profile differs by \(0.061\,\%\) of \(\rho_L\), while the total error is \(0.13\,\%\). Time error and space error partly cancel at this step. Because the step is halved with the cell size, both shrink together in the grid study.
Wave positions. On the finest grid the shock is \(0.06\) cells and the border of the two gases \(0.35\) cells from their exact places. The two ends of the fan, read from the straight-line fit, are \(1.4\) and \(1.1\) cells off; the back of the fan is about one cell too far right on every grid (\(0.98\) to \(1.05\) cells), so that error halves with the cell size.

theorylogged 2026-11-15 08:44
Problem
SymPy could not prove that the expansion fan satisfies the Euler equations. Four self-checks came back false although the formulas were right.
Diagnosis
The fan's density and pressure are powers of a base with the exponent 2/(gamma-1), kept symbolic. The base was written as an expression in the ray variable, whose sign SymPy does not know, so it refused to combine the powers and the residual did not reduce to zero.
Fix
The fan is written with the local speed of sound divided by the left one as the variable, declared positive. The three conservation laws then reduce to zero for any gamma. The entropy check compares logarithms.
Lesson
A symbolic proof with non-integer powers needs a base that is a single positive symbol. A check that fails this way says nothing about the physics.
Guideline
In the workflow's theory stage, add one line. When a solution has powers with a symbolic exponent (gas dynamics, similarity solutions), write it in a variable that is a positive symbol before asking SymPy to verify it.

Problem
OpenFOAM's universal gas constant is not the textbook value, and the tutorial's molar mass of air (28.96) does not give the gas constant of the problem. Typed from memory, the start densities would be wrong by a few hundredths of a per cent.
Diagnosis
The solver divides its own universal gas constant by the molar mass. The constant is built into the program as a product of two rounded numbers; a one-step run gave \(8314.47006651\ \mathrm{J/(kmol\,K)}\), where textbooks give 8314.4626. With the tutorial's 28.96 the gas constant is 287.10, not 287.
Fix
run.py measures the constant with a 10-cell, one-step run (p W / (rho T) in an undisturbed cell) and sets the molar mass from it. The pre-flight checks that the solver's start densities are the given ones to 12 digits.
Lesson
For a compressible case the density is not an input. It follows from pressure, temperature and the solver's own constants, so it has to be checked in the solver's output before anything else.
Guideline
Add to the OpenFOAM primer, in a new section on compressible flow, that molWeight must be set from the solver's own universal gas constant, with the measured value for v2606 and the one-step check of the start density. A helper in cfdlib.foam that returns the constant would save every later case of the ladder this step.

Problem
The workflow does not say what to compare a finite-volume result with when the solution has jumps. The exact value at a cell centre is the wrong reference in a cell that a jump passes through.
Diagnosis
The solver holds cell averages of density, momentum and energy. Next to a jump the exact cell average and the exact centre value differ by up to half the jump, and the error this adds is as large as the error being measured (a few cells times the jump).
Fix
The exact solution is integrated over each cell in closed form, and velocity and pressure are formed from the averaged conserved quantities as the solver forms them. Every reading rule (states, positions, widths) is also applied to these exact cell averages, which gives its bias (up to 0.11 cells for a jump's position, 1.3 to 1.6 cells for a jump's width).
Lesson
With jumps, compare cell averages with cell averages, and use the mean error (L1), not the largest one.
Guideline
Add to the workflow's analysis stage, for flows with shocks. Compare with exact cell averages of the conserved quantities, judge profiles by the L1 error, and measure each extraction rule on the exact solution. cases/shock_tube_sod/theory.py (Exact.cell_averages) and measure.py are the worked example.

The total variation above the exact one falls from 12 % to 2 %, 1 % and 0.9 %.
Fix
The main runs use a usual Courant number of 0.19 (0.097 as the solver prints it), half the tutorial's step, where the ripple is 0.6 %. A limit on the ripple and on the total variation is part of the acceptance rules.
Lesson
The time step of rhoCentralFoam is set by the ringing its Euler step makes, not by stability alone. The error against the exact solution is not a good guide here; it was lowest at 0.19 only because the time error and the space error partly cancel.
Guideline
Add to the primer that rhoCentralFoam needs a usual Courant number of about 0.2 or less (maxCo 0.1 in its own measure) for a clean shock, with these measured numbers, and that a total-variation check of a profile that should be monotone is a cheap detector. A tool for the ladder would be a solver with a multi-stage time scheme.
