  • The answer. Ignition at \(217.709\,\mathrm{\mu s}\) (reference \(217.77\,\mathrm{\mu s}\), \(-0.028\,\%\)); temperature at the end \(2220.54\,\mathrm{K}\) (reference \(2220.5\,\mathrm{K}\)). Both are far inside the required accuracy.
  • Three programs, one answer. OpenFOAM, extrapolated to a time step of zero, and the independent program written for this report agree on the ignition time to \(3 \times 10^{-7}\) (relative) and on the final temperature to \(3 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{K}\), when both use OpenFOAM's constants. So OpenFOAM integrates this reaction scheme correctly.
  • Most of the small difference from the reference belongs to the reference. The independent program with CHEMKIN's own constants ignites \(0.022\,\%\) before the reference, and its answer does not move when its tolerances are tightened. The reference states no tolerances. OpenFOAM's different constants add \(0.0057\,\%\) on the ignition time and \(+0.09\,\mathrm{K}\) on the final temperature.
  • The time step matters, to first order. Inside a step OpenFOAM lets the gas react without expanding, and corrects the density only after the step. The ignition time is early in proportion to the step (observed order \(0.99\), Figure 4): halve the step, halve the error.
  • Atoms and energy are kept to round-off at every step, as the method is built to do. The check is made outside OpenFOAM, from the printed temperature and composition.
  • What it costs. A step costs about \(123\) microseconds, except during the rise, where a tight tolerance costs \(2.5\) s in total whatever the step. The explicit method RKF45 gave the same answer as the tutorial's implicit one in \(0.29\) of the time.

1 Problem

A uniform mixture of hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen at rest is suddenly at 1000 K. Nothing flows and no heat is exchanged; the pressure is held constant. After a short delay the mixture ignites and burns. Find the temperature against time, the ignition time and the final temperature, and compare them with a reference calculation made with the same reaction scheme by an independent program.

What we want to find

  1. aTemperature against time up to \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\), compared with the reference
  2. bIgnition time, compared with the reference
  3. cTemperature at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\), compared with the reference
  4. dHow the amounts of hydrogen, oxygen, water and the short-lived species OH, H and O change in time
  5. eDo the numbers of hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen atoms stay constant, and does the energy balance of a parcel at constant pressure hold?
  6. fHow the answers depend on the accuracy settings of the chemistry solver and on the time step, and what one such parcel costs per time step

Required accuracy (given with the problem)

  • Ignition time within 1 % of the reference
  • Temperature at the end within 0.1 % of the reference
  • The number of atoms of each element constant within 0.001 %

Setup

  • A closed, well-mixed parcel of gas: every property is the same everywhere, so there is no flow and only time matters.
  • Start: temperature \(T_0\), pressure \(p\), mixture of hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen in the mole ratio \(1 : 1 : 3.76\).
  • The pressure stays at \(p\) (the parcel is free to expand) and no heat is exchanged with the surroundings.
  • The gases are ideal gases whose heat capacities change with temperature.
  • Reaction scheme and thermodynamic data: the hydrogen scheme that ships with OpenFOAM, files `chem.inp` and `therm.dat` in `$FOAM_TUTORIALS/combustion/chemFoam/h2/chemkin/` (10 species, 27 reaction lines; argon is listed and absent here). Use these files as they are.
  • Ignition time: the time at which the temperature first reaches \(T_{\mathrm{ign}}\), the criterion of the reference.
  • Reference: the calculation of the program CHEMKIN II (SENKIN) for exactly these conditions, shipped with OpenFOAM in the same folder (`senk.out`).

3 Theory

No formula gives the temperature against time: the 27 reactions between 9 species are a set of coupled equations in which some species change a million times faster than others. They can only be solved step by step. Theory still fixes a good deal before any run: the start state; two things that cannot change, the number of atoms of each element and the enthalpy (the energy of a parcel at constant pressure); two limits for the final temperature; and the shape of the history (a slow start in which the temperature difference grows like an exponential, then a sudden rise). The equations themselves are written out below and solved here a second time by an independent program (mech.py: its own reading of the two data files, the rate laws of the CHEMKIN manual, SciPy's stiff integrators with a relative tolerance of \(1 \times 10^{-10}\)). That gives a second answer next to the reference, with known tolerances, and a tool to measure what a difference in the constants of two programs is worth.

The parcel
\[ \sigma_i = \frac{\text{moles of species } i}{\text{kg of gas}},\qquad v = \frac{R\,T}{p}\sum_i \sigma_i,\qquad c_i = \frac{\sigma_i}{v} \]
One kilogram of gas is followed. Its volume \(v\) follows from the ideal-gas law at the fixed pressure, so the gas expands as it heats and as the number of molecules changes. \(c_i\) is the concentration that the reaction rates need.
Each reaction
\[ q_j = k_{f,j}\prod_i c_i^{\nu'_{ij}} - k_{r,j}\prod_i c_i^{\nu''_{ij}},\qquad k_f = A\,T^{\beta}\,e^{-E/(R T)},\qquad k_r = \frac{k_f}{K_c},\quad K_c = e^{-\Delta G^{\circ}/(R T)}\left(\frac{p^{\circ}}{R T}\right)^{\Delta\nu} \]
Rate of reaction \(j\): forward minus backward. \(A\), \(\beta\), \(E\) come from chem.inp. The backward rate is not given: it follows from the forward rate and the thermodynamic data (therm.dat), so that the reactions stop exactly at chemical equilibrium. \(p^{\circ}\) is the pressure for which the data are tabulated. Reactions that need a third molecule to carry energy away multiply by the concentration of such molecules; four reactions of the scheme change between a low-pressure and a high-pressure form (fall-off).
Species
\[ \frac{d\sigma_i}{dt} = v\,\omega_i,\qquad \omega_i = \sum_j (\nu''_{ij} - \nu'_{ij})\,q_j \]
Moles of each species per kilogram change by what the reactions make and use.
Energy at constant pressure, no heat exchange
\[ \sum_i \sigma_i h_i(T) = h_0 = \text{const}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dT}{dt} = -\,\frac{v\sum_i h_i\,\omega_i}{\sum_i \sigma_i c_{p,i}} \]
\(h_i\) is the enthalpy of a mole of species \(i\), chemical energy included. The total cannot change, so when the reactions turn chemical energy into heat the temperature rises. Checked with SymPy: with this \(dT/dt\) the time derivative of the sum is zero.
Atoms
\[ \sum_i a_{ie}\,\sigma_i = \text{const for each element } e \]
\(a_{ie}\) is the number of atoms of element \(e\) in a molecule of species \(i\). Reactions only rearrange atoms.
Where it ends: chemical equilibrium
\[ \min_{\sigma}\ \sum_i \sigma_i\,\mu_i(T, p, \sigma)\quad\text{with the atoms and } h_0 \text{ fixed} \]
Left alone for long enough, the mixture settles where its Gibbs energy is lowest. That state depends only on the thermodynamic data, not on the reaction rates, and gives the temperature that the history must approach.
  • SymPy: with the energy equation above, the enthalpy of the parcel does not change in time
  • SymPy: in the data's polynomials the enthalpy is the integral of the heat capacity, and the entropy of heat capacity over temperature
  • Each of the 27 reactions, as read from the file, has the same atoms on both sides
  • The independent solution keeps the atoms of every element to \(4 \times 10^{-15}\) and the enthalpy to \(3 \times 10^{-8}\) of the heat released
  • Two integrators (Radau, LSODA) and three tolerances give the same ignition time to \(8 \times 10^{-11}\) (relative) and the same final temperature to \(6 \times 10^{-10}\) K
  • The rate equations, run for one second, arrive at the equilibrium found by minimising the Gibbs energy (difference \(6 \times 10^{-12}\) K): rates and thermodynamic data are consistent
  • The final temperature lies between the history's end and the limit of complete burning
  • The start density agrees with the one the reference prints (\(0.58967\) \(\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), five digits)
  • The data's two temperature ranges join at 1000 K, the start temperature: the largest jump of a heat capacity there is \(7.5 \times 10^{-5}\,\%\) (N2) and of an enthalpy \(0.0032\) K times the gas constant per mole (H2O2); small enough not to matter here

D6
Fixed time steps, a family of six with ratio 2 from \(0.4\) to \(0.0125\,\mathrm{\mu s}\) (2500 to 80000 steps); the answers are those of the smallest step, with the value extrapolated to a step of zero next to them
Accuracy
Why this choice
The time step matters here in a way that is easy to miss. Inside one step chemFoam hands the gas to the chemistry solver, which integrates the reactions accurately but with the density frozen: the gas does not expand while it reacts. Only after the step are the temperature and the density brought up to date from the enthalpy. So during a step the molecules are a little too close together, the reactions a little too fast, and the error is in proportion to the step (first order; Euler is the matching first-order scheme for the mass fractions, and a single cell has nothing else to discretise). A family with ratio 2 shows that order and allows the extrapolation. The tutorial instead lets the step follow the chemistry solver's own sub-step (adjustTimeStep on); that way is studied as a variant, with the tutorial's maxDeltaT 1, which never limits anything.
Effect on the result
The ignition time and every temperature: with \(0.4\,\mathrm{\mu s}\) steps the ignition is \(0.018\,\%\) early, and the error halves with the step (pre-flight). The time scale to resolve is \(1/\lambda = 7.6\,\mathrm{\mu s}\) (Theory).
Alternatives
The tutorial's adjustable step: fewer steps, but the step then depends on the tolerance of the chemistry solver, so the two error sources cannot be told apart. A second-order treatment of the expansion: chemFoam has none.

Accuracy
Why this choice
Reactions between radicals are thousands of times faster than the overall burning (a stiff problem): an explicit method would need tiny sub-steps, an implicit one does not. seulex is the tutorial's choice. Its tolerances are set tight for the family so that the family measures the time step alone: the tutorial's relative tolerance is \(0.1\). The absolute tolerance matters in the slow start, when the reactive species are still at concentrations of \(10^{-12}\,\mathrm{kmol/m^3}\) and less and the ignition time is being decided. The study then answers what looser settings cost in accuracy and save in time. initialChemicalTimeStep is only the first sub-step tried. Effect on the result
How accurately each step's chemistry is integrated, and most of the cost: around ignition one step at the tight tolerance costs many times a step of the slow start (pre-flight; the numbers are in the performance appendix).

8 Workflow insights

Problems met while producing this report, logged at the moment they happened, with how they were solved and what future agents should do differently. These feed back into the workflow guidelines.

setuplogged 2026-11-15 13:06
Problem
OpenFOAM's tutorial for this very problem (combustion/chemFoam/h2), run as shipped, takes 47 time steps for the whole millisecond. Its temperature at the end is close to the reference, but 47 points cannot show when the mixture ignites.
Diagnosis
The tutorial sets adjustTimeStep on, and chemFoam then makes each time step equal to the last sub-step of its chemistry solver. With the tutorial's loose tolerance (0.1) those sub-steps are long. Reading the source showed a second thing: inside a time step the chemistry is integrated with the density frozen, and the gas expands only after the step. So the time step is an accuracy setting in its own right, first order, and the tutorial hides it behind the tolerance.
Fix
The answers come from fixed time steps, a family of six with ratio 2, with tight tolerances of the chemistry solver. The tutorial's way is kept as a variant study with six tolerances. The family showed first order (observed order 0.99 and 1.00) and the extrapolation to a step of zero lands on the independent solution.
Lesson
For a solver that splits chemistry from the rest, the outer time step sets an error that no tolerance of the chemistry solver removes. A tutorial that passes a visual comparison says nothing about that error.
Guideline
Reference note for reacting cases: "The time step of chemFoam, and of every OpenFOAM solver that calls the chemistry library once per step, is an accuracy setting of first order. Study it with fixed steps and tight chemistry tolerances first; study the tolerances after." It belongs in a reference on combustion, not in the main workflow text.
setuplogged 2026-11-15 13:06
Problem
At start-up chemFoam prints a warning that the sum of the mass fractions differs from one by 9. It looks like a wrong mixture.
Diagnosis
Harmless. chemFoam writes its own start files, with every one of the ten species set to 1 (0/Ydefault: uniform 1), so their sum is 10. The library warns and rescales; right after, the solver replaces the values by the mixture of constant/initialConditions.
Fix
No change to the case. The pre-flight has a check that does not trust the warning either way: after the first step, the density, the enthalpy and the three mass fractions must equal the start state of Theory (they do, to nine digits and better).
Lesson
A warning about the start mixture is settled by comparing the solver's first state with the hand-computed start state, not by reading the warning.
Guideline
A line for the list of known harmless messages (if cfdlib.monitor keeps one): "chemFoam: Sum of mass fractions is different from one ... differs from 1 by (number of species minus one): comes from the start files the solver writes for itself". And a pre-flight check for every reacting case: first state against the start state of Theory.

Problem
chemFoam writes its history file chemFoam.out with 6 significant digits, and writes the species only at the written times. A study of how the answer changes with the time step needs more digits and the species at every step.
Diagnosis
The file follows writePrecision, whose default is 6. The differences between time steps here are in the sixth to ninth digit. Species are fields, and fields are written only at write times.

  • What a step costs. Away from the rise, a step costs about \(123\) microseconds whatever the tolerance: the chemistry solver has almost nothing to do, and the time goes to calling it, updating the gas properties and writing a log line and two output rows. This is an upper limit for what the chemistry of one cell costs in a flow solver, where no line is printed per cell.
  • The rise is the expensive part with tight tolerances. With an absolute tolerance of \(1 \times 10^{-16}\) the rise costs \(2.3\) to \(2.7\) s in every run of the family, whether it is cut into 126 steps or 4032: the solver's sub-steps are set by the tolerance, not by the time step (Figure 6, left). With the tutorial's tolerances the same rise costs \(0.05\) s.
  • Cheaper at the same accuracy. RKF45, an explicit method, gave the ignition time of seulex to nine digits in \(1.4\) s against \(4.9\) s. With steps this small the problem is not stiff within a step, so an implicit method pays for a strength it does not use. With larger steps that would turn around.
  • Cheapest run inside the required accuracy: the tutorial as shipped, 47 steps and \(0.01\) s inside the time loop. For an accuracy of \(0.04\,\%\) the adjustable step with a relative tolerance of \(1 \times 10^{-4}\) needs 353 steps.
  • The table of species written at every step costs little: \(4.86\) s with it, \(4.73\) s without.
  • Run times repeat only roughly. The run with the smallest step took \(21.2\) s the first time, while another job was using the machine, and \(11.0\) s when repeated alone; the tables show the repeat. All runs together took \(72\) s of solver time.
  • For a flow with chemistry in every cell (the later steps towards a detonation engine), these numbers say: about a tenth of a millisecond of one core per cell per step where little reacts, and ten to a hundred times that in cells that are igniting if tolerances are tight. A million cells would need about \(123\) core-seconds per step. Not tried here: methods that tabulate or reduce the chemistry, and running it on the GPU.
