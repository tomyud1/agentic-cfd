Key findings

  • It is a detonation, and it travels at the right speed. On every grid the pressure front and the burning front move together, and the speed over the second half of the time is \(-0.49\,\%\), \(-0.32\,\%\), \(-0.41\,\%\), \(-0.51\,\%\) from the Chapman-Jouguet speed (400, 800, 1600, 3200 cells).
  • The speed does not need a resolved front. None of the grids resolves the stretch in which the gas waits before it burns (\(0.17\,\mathrm{mm}\); the finest grid has \(4.6\) cells in it). The speed is fixed by conservation and by the heat released, which a conservative run gets right on any grid. What the coarse grids miss is the pressure peak at the front: \(0.63\) of the shock pressure on the coarsest grid, \(0.79\) on the finest.
  • Energy conservation is the setting that decides. With one pass through the equations per time step, as in OpenFOAM's reacting examples, the solver loses energy and the wave runs \(17.4\,\%\) too slowly (energy lost over the second half: \(15.3\,\%\) of the heat released). Five passes per step bring the loss down to \(0.21\,\%\). The loss does not show in residuals or in the mass.
  • The burnt state has no plateau. In a closed tube an expansion wave follows the front at once, so the pressure falls continuously. Read where the gas leaves the front at its own speed of sound, the state is \(-4.4\,\%\) in pressure and \(-1.2\,\%\) in temperature from the CJ state. 1 Problem

A closed tube holds hydrogen and air at rest. A thin layer of the same gas at one end is suddenly hot and at high pressure. It sends a shock down the tube that ignites the gas behind it: a detonation. Find the speed of the wave and the state of the burnt gas, and compare them with the Chapman-Jouguet theory, which fixes both from the gas alone.

What we want to find

  1. aSpeed of the wave, compared with the Chapman-Jouguet speed of the reference
  2. bIs it a detonation: do the pressure front and the burning front travel together, and how far apart are they?
  3. cPressure and temperature of the burnt gas just behind the wave, compared with the Chapman-Jouguet state
  4. dThe highest pressure at the front, compared with the pressure behind a shock of that speed without burning, and how it changes with the cell size
  5. eAre the mass in the tube and the number of atoms of each element kept, and how well is the total energy kept?
  6. fHow the speed depends on the cell size and the time step, and the cost per cell and time step with the share of the chemistry

Required accuracy (given with the problem)

  • Wave speed within 2 % of the Chapman-Jouguet speed of the reference
  • From 10 microseconds on, the burning front stays within 1 mm of the pressure front

Setup

  • A straight tube of length \(L\), closed at both ends. Only the distance along the tube matters (one dimension).
  • Start: hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen in the mole ratio \(2 : 1 : 3.76\), at rest, at \(p_0\) and \(T_0\) everywhere.
  • Ignition: a layer of thickness \(L_{\mathrm{ign}}\) at the left end holds the same mixture at \(T_{\mathrm{ign}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{ign}}\) at the start.
  • The gases are ideal gases whose heat capacities change with temperature. Friction and heat conduction play no part in the answer.
  • Reaction scheme and thermodynamic data: the hydrogen scheme that ships with OpenFOAM, files `chem.inp` and `therm.dat` in `$FOAM_TUTORIALS/combustion/chemFoam/h2/chemkin/` (10 species, 27 reaction lines). Use these files as they are.
  • The wave speed is the speed of the pressure front over the second half of the time, \(t_{\mathrm{end}}/2\) to \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\).

3 Theory

Theory gives much of the answer here, because a detonation that has settled travels as a steady wave. Three conservation laws across the wave, the thermodynamic data of the gas and one extra condition fix its speed and the state behind it. This is the Chapman-Jouguet (CJ) state of the reference file, and it does not depend on how fast the reactions are. The same laws give the state right behind the leading shock before anything has burnt, and, with the reaction rates of the scheme, how far the gas travels before it burns (the steady structure, known as the ZND model). In a closed tube the burnt gas must come to rest at the closed end, which fixes the expansion wave that follows the front and the pressure at the end. All of this is computed here with cfdlib.chem from the scheme's own two files and without the flow solver. What theory does not give: how the wave forms out of the ignition layer and how long it takes to settle, and whether the steady wave is stable. Those are what the simulation is for.

Conservation across a steady wave
\[ \rho_1 D = \rho_2 w,\qquad p_1 + \rho_1 D^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 w^2,\qquad h_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}D^2 = h_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}w^2 \]
Seen by someone travelling with the wave, fresh gas (1) flows in at the wave speed \(D\) and burnt gas (2) leaves at the speed \(w\). Mass, momentum and energy that flow in must flow out. The enthalpy \(h\) includes the chemical energy, so the heat of burning is inside \(h_1 - h_2\).
Solved for the state behind the wave
\[ p_2 = p_1 + \rho_1^2 D^2\,(v_1 - v_2),\qquad h_2 - h_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(p_2 - p_1)\,(v_1 + v_2),\qquad v = 1/\rho \]
The first relation is a straight line in pressure against volume whose slope is set by the wave speed. The second no longer contains the speed: it is the curve of all states that burnt gas can reach. Both were derived from the three laws with SymPy.
The extra condition (Chapman-Jouguet)
\[ w = c_2\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad D_{\mathrm{CJ}} = \min_{v_2} D \]
A wave that nothing pushes from behind settles at the lowest speed for which a burnt state exists. At that speed the burnt gas leaves the wave at exactly its own speed of sound \(c_2\), so nothing that happens behind the wave can catch up with it. The burnt gas is taken in chemical equilibrium (the composition of lowest Gibbs energy at its pressure and enthalpy).
Right behind the shock, before burning
\[ \text{the same three laws with the composition of the fresh gas} \]
The leading shock compresses and heats the gas faster than any reaction. This state has the highest pressure of the steady wave, well above the CJ pressure; the pressure then falls to the CJ value as the gas burns and speeds up.
The steady structure with the reaction rates
\[ \frac{d\sigma_i}{dt} = v\,\omega_i(\sigma, T, v),\qquad p = p_1 + \rho_1^2 D^2 (v_1 - v),\qquad h(\sigma, T) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\rho_1 D v)^2 = h_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}D^2,\qquad \frac{dx}{dt} = \rho_1 D\,v \]
A parcel is followed from the shock on. \(\sigma_i\) are its moles of each species per kilogram and \(\omega_i\) the rates of the 27 reactions. Pressure and temperature follow from the two conservation relations at every instant, and \(x\) is the distance behind the shock. For a while almost nothing happens (the reactive species multiply unseen), then the gas burns quickly.
The expansion behind the wave in a closed tube
\[ u - \int \frac{dp}{\rho\,c} = \text{const},\qquad \frac{x}{t} = u + c,\qquad u = 0 \text{ at the closed end} \]
The burnt gas leaves the front moving forward at \(u_{\mathrm{CJ}}\), but the end wall is at rest. An expansion wave slows it down. In this wave each state travels at its own fixed speed \(u + c\), so the profile stretches in proportion to time, and the gas is at rest between the wall and the point \(x = c_{\mathrm{end}}\,t\). Computed along the path of constant entropy with the gas in chemical equilibrium.
What the closed tube keeps
\[ \frac{d}{dt}\int \rho\,dV = 0,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\int \rho\,Y_i\,\frac{a_{ie}}{W_i}\,dV = 0,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\int \left(\rho\,h + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,u^2 - p\right) dV = 0 \]
Mass, the atoms of each element (\(a_{ie}\) atoms of element \(e\) in species \(i\)) and total energy: nothing enters or leaves, no wall moves and no heat is exchanged. The solver is built to keep the first two. It is not built to keep the third, so the energy balance is the physical check of every run.
  • The three conservation laws are satisfied by the solved relations
  • Pressure behind the wave: the straight line in pressure and volume (Rayleigh line)
  • Enthalpy behind the wave
  • The speed drops out: enthalpy rise = half the pressure rise times the sum of the volumes (Hugoniot)
  • The CJ state of the reference file satisfies the three conservation relations with this report's enthalpies
  • The CJ state computed here reproduces the reference file: speed to \(2 \times 10^{-7}\), pressure to \(2 \times 10^{-8}\), temperature to \(9 \times 10^{-7}\) (relative)
  • The state right behind the shock reproduces the reference file: pressure to \(3 \times 10^{-7}\), temperature to \(3 \times 10^{-6}\)
  • The steady structure ends at the CJ state: \(21\,\mathrm{mm}\) behind the shock its pressure is \(+0.72\,\%\) and its temperature \(+0.08\,\%\) from the CJ values
  • The speed of sound found along the expansion path equals the speed at which the burnt gas leaves the wave: \(1097.8\) against \(1098.1\,\mathrm{m/s}\)
  • Along the expansion, pressure, temperature and density fall together, and the gas comes to rest at \(0.378\) of the CJ pressure

Why this choice

These are the schemes of OpenFOAM's reacting examples. The limiter keeps a jump from making new peaks. Time step
D9
A fixed time step on each grid, such that the wave crosses about 0.4 of a cell per step (\(56\) down to \(7.6\) nanoseconds), reached from a tiny first step; twice and half that step are run on one grid
Accuracy
Why this choice
The step of a run is maxDeltaT. The run does not start with it: the first step is \(10^{-12}\,\mathrm{s}\) and each step is a fifth longer than the one before until the step of the run is reached, after about 50 steps and six steps' worth of time. The reason is a start-up fault of the solver: on its first step it has no stored gas state of the step before, and a first step of full size, in which the hot layer reacts, created 0.17 % of mass (insight 7). maxCo 100 is the solver's own limit for the growing step and is never reached. The solver shortens a step slightly to land on each sampling time, so the states along the tube are written exactly every \(0.5\) microseconds, among them the two times of the wave speed. The size is a compromise found in trials: the energy lost per step falls in proportion to the time step and with the passes per step, and 0.4 of a cell with five passes costs the same as 0.2 with three while losing a third as much. The chemistry is integrated inside each step with sub-steps of its own, so the flow step need not resolve the fastest reactions (\(10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}\)); it must resolve the wait before burning (\(0.45\) microseconds: 8 to 60 steps). The end time is given; nothing becomes steady in a closed tube.
Effect on the result
The coupling of flow and chemistry (first order in the step), the energy balance and the cost.
Alternatives
A step that follows the flow's Courant number: the step would change with the grid's smearing of the front, and a time-step study would be harder to read. A fixed step from the first step on: not mass-conserving at the start.

Passes per time step
D10
Five passes through the equations per time step (nOuterCorrectors 5), two pressure corrections per pass, momentum solved first
Accuracy
Why this choice
In each pass the solver solves momentum, species, energy and then pressure. The energy equation needs the rate of change of the pressure, and in the first pass it only has the value of the step before. With one pass per step (the setting of OpenFOAM's reacting examples) the tube lost energy equal to 10 % of the heat released, and the wave ran 9 % too slowly; a finer grid did not help. Repeating the pass with the new pressure removes the error: 0.8 % with three passes, 0.13 % with five (trials on 1200 cells at half this time step). Even numbers of passes add energy at the start (insight 4), so the count is odd. The pre-flight repeats the comparison as a study.
Effect on the result
Whether total energy is kept, and so the wave speed. Also the cost: the chemistry is solved again in every pass.
Alternatives
One pass with a time step ten times smaller: more costly for the same energy error. Stopping the passes on a residual (outerCorrectorResidualControl): the count would change from step to step, and with it the even-odd effect.

7 Comparison

Verification against theory: the Chapman-Jouguet speed and state of the reference file follow from conservation and the thermodynamic data alone, and the simulated wave must settle to them. The steady structure and the expansion wave of Theory stand next to the simulated profiles.

How the differences are measured

Wave speed
\[ D = \frac{x_p(t_{\mathrm{end}}) - x_p(t_{\mathrm{end}}/2)}{t_{\mathrm{end}}/2} \]
\(x_p\): the pressure front, the furthest point where the pressure is halfway between the start pressure and the CJ pressure, on a straight line between two cell centres.
Distance between the fronts
\[ x_p - x_b \]
\(x_b\): the burning front, the furthest point where the water mass fraction is half its CJ value. Judged at each of the sampled states (every half microsecond) from 10 microseconds on.
Burnt state
\[ u + c = D \]
The first point behind the front where the gas (speed \(u\), speed of sound \(c\) at fixed composition) moves away from the front at its own speed of sound. Nothing behind that point can reach the front; in the ideal wave it is the CJ state. The CJ state of the reference uses the speed of sound of gas that stays in chemical equilibrium, \(1098\,\mathrm{m/s}\); at fixed composition it is \(1134\,\mathrm{m/s}\) for the same state. With the lower value the point would lie closer to the front, where the pressure is higher, so this reading gives a pressure on the low side.
Highest pressure at the front
\[ \frac{\overline{p_{\max}}}{p_{\mathrm{s}}(D)} \]
The highest pressure in the tube, averaged over every step of the second half of the time, over the pressure behind a shock of the simulated speed \(D\) in the unburnt gas.
Change of total energy
\[ \frac{E(t) - E(0)}{q(t)} \]
\(E\): total energy in the tube; \(q\): heat released by the burning up to time \(t\). For the second half of the time both differences are taken over that half.

Problem
The chemistry takes about 97 % of the run time of reactingFoam on this problem, and it costs the same in every cell, also in the cold gas that the wave has not reached. Every chemistry method costs about the same.
Diagnosis
OpenFOAM integrates the chemistry of every cell in every time step, at least one step of the method per cell: about \(21\) to \(33\) microseconds per cell, whatever the cell holds. The flow part of a step costs under \(1\) microsecond per cell. Five methods and tolerances were timed on the same short run: the explicit method RKF45 was cheapest (\(25\,\mathrm{s}\)), seulex dearest (\(40\,\mathrm{s}\)), and all put the front within \(0.02\,\mathrm{mm}\) of each other after \(19\,\mathrm{mm}\) of travel.

Lesson
In a flow solver the chemistry is not dear where it is fast; it is dear everywhere, because of the fixed cost per cell and step. With time steps of a few nanoseconds no cell is stiff, so an explicit method is enough.
Guideline
The primer's chemistry section could say: in reactingFoam budget about \(20\) to \(30\) microseconds per cell and step for the hydrogen scheme on one core, in every cell; Treact skips cold cells; the chemistry is solved again in every outer corrector.

Problem
With the settings of OpenFOAM's reacting examples (one pass per time step, called PISO mode) the wave ran at about \(1790\,\mathrm{m/s}\) on the first grid, 9 % below the Chapman-Jouguet speed, and a finer grid at the same Courant number was no better.

The solver was losing energy. The total energy in the closed tube (heat content, chemical energy and kinetic energy minus pressure times volume, summed from the solver's own fields after every step) fell by about 10 % of the heat the burning had released. Halving the time step halved the loss (5 %); halving the cell size at the same Courant number did not reduce it (12 %). The cause is the order of the solver's steps: the energy equation is solved before the pressure, with the pressure change of the pass before. Five passes per time step, and the energy balance of every run as the physical check of the plan, with a limit set before the runs.

A pressure-based solver does not conserve energy by construction. On a detonation the missing energy is missing heat release, and the wave is slower: in the full runs on 800 cells it is 17 % below the CJ speed with one pass (15 % of the heat lost), 0.6 % below with three passes (1.6 % lost) and 0.3 % below with five (0.2 % lost). It does not show in residuals, in the mass or in the look of the profiles, and grid refinement does not remove it.
Guideline
The primer needs an entry for pressure-based compressible solvers with shocks or burning (reactingFoam, rhoPimpleFoam): PISO mode loses energy in proportion to the time step; use 3 to 5 outer correctors and check the energy balance. On one core over the first fifth of the time (800 cells), the run took \(23.0\) s with chemistry and \(4.0\) s without, although only \(8\,\%\) of the cell-steps were burnt cells. That is \(151\) microseconds per burnt cell and pass for the chemistry, against \(2.6\) per cell and pass for the flow. The last column but one applies this flow cost to each run: what is left is chemistry and, on 8 ranks, the cost of message passing.
  • Why the chemistry is dear. Every burnt cell is integrated again in each of the five passes of a step, with an explicit method that needs many sub-steps in hot gas near equilibrium. The implicit method is no way out here: the same run with seulex gave the same wave speed (\(-0.32\,\%\)) and took \(7.0\) times as long (\(513\) s against \(73\) s). A looser tolerance of the explicit method saved \(13\,\%\) of the time for the same speed (\(-0.33\,\%\)). Without the cut-off temperature every cell is integrated in every pass, burnt or not: \(87\) s against \(73\) s.
  • Parallel runs gain little on small grids. In short trial runs before the plan was fixed (insight 11; not among the runs of this table), a step on 8 ranks cost about 25 milliseconds more than the work it held, on 400 cells as on 1200, so on 1200 cells 8 ranks were only twice as fast as one core. Without the cut into stretches they were no faster at all, because all the burnt gas sat in the first ranks. In the runs of this table the same shows as the cost per burnt cell and pass: \(151\) microseconds on one core, \(205\) core-microseconds on 8 ranks (800 cells).
  • What would make it faster. Solving the chemistry once per step with the heat release held over the passes (not possible in the stock solver); a density-based solver that conserves energy in one pass; tabulated or reduced chemistry; the chemistry of all cells on the GPU. None was tried here: the task was to solve with the stock solver.
